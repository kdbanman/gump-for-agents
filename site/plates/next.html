What is not settled, and what is running.

  1. Can gump compute? It has wires, pulses, a gate, a clock of any length, a gun, an echo, a stream of nine known signals, a line that turns itself into a register holding the number of holes, and a memory cell: pulses write a count, a crossing wire reads one unit without disturbing it, and a pair of pulses on a crossing wire calls the count back out as that many pulses, which another store catches (proved for every count; the proof not yet refereed). In the reviewer’s words it is a hand-operated accumulator: set, add one, move-and-clear into the left neighbour, clear, and an emptiness test; no copy, doubling, subtraction, comparison or parity. Open, in order: a decrement that reports zero and leaves the count where it can be decremented again (the one-less collision does it once, hand-timed); the one-less collision at the oscillator’s one-pulse call; any operation started by a signal the machine made itself, starting with a source for the calling pair; schedules that respect the hazards; a referee for the proof of the call. Also: a write with a reader in place. The ledger: research/OPEN-PROBLEMS.md, item 13, (v), (y) and (aa) to (ae). open
  2. A clock whose period divides by four. Every built clock has period 4N + 2. Half the reason is found: the remainder belongs to the two ends, and every end that fits inside the wire’s tube has the same one. Ends of the other kind exist one size up, and each destroys itself turning the shuttle round. Why the class is forced is not known. open
  3. Why the fade costs more. There is no single price per cell: a face with a rim pays about 0.231, a wide wrapped face 0.2087, a tube less. Behind them are two ways of dying: a wrapped face over-fills and crashes, a face with a rim fades, because its rim row has fewer neighbours inside the box (“Two ways to die”). That is a mechanism; no price follows from it. Open: why the fade costs more per cell than the crash, as a calculation (9 (n)); why a hull face with k cells left dies less often than a wrapped one (not the crash, not the rim row; the layer beneath is fuller, untested as the cause, 9 (p)); why a tube, over-full as often as a wide face, dies 1.25 times as often at 84 cells (9 (q)). The hull’s rare crashes are not a second, cheaper way to die within reach: their share is flat from 63 to 84 cells (9 (o), one code). The ledger: research/OPEN-PROBLEMS.md, item 9 (n) to (q). open
  4. Why a way round costs what it does. A living face is a little emptier for every way round it, by an amount that falls smoothly as the way round lengthens. A tube’s price per cell falls as it grows: 0.2044 from 54 to 84 cells, 0.2020 from 84 to 108, and 0.1957 from 108 to 120; tubes of girth √13 bend too, girth 4 leans. Running on your card: the 126- and 132-cell tubes, with the lead’s forecasts W14 to W16 frozen before any row. Open: the form of a long tube’s lifetime, and whether girth 4 bends (9 (l)); whether a loop of √21 costs anything at all (on two codes the face is midway between the two readings; the lean recorded on 2026-11-16 is withdrawn, 9 (m)). open · running
  5. Why the thinnest face costs half. Same rule, same five layers of depth, half the price. Nothing explains the ratio. open
  6. What a face remembers. Copies of one state, each with a single cell-orbit flipped, live a little longer when the original did. Something slow in the state sets its odds by a fifth or so, and nobody knows what. seen once, weakly
  7. One sixth. Whether a symmetric face’s cost tends to exactly one sixth of an ordinary face’s as it grows. The data allow it and do not yet demand it. open
